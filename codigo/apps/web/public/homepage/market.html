Check a market where you know nobody

The first request we received came from a maker abroad. They wanted photos of competitors on shelves: are they there, and what do the tags say? They knew nobody in that market and had no way to judge a stranger's photo.

That is the problem from the other side of the counter. A brand analysing a market it does not know needs a few facts from a few shops, quickly, from someone it has no reason to trust. Shelfie turns that into a single order: one product, one store, one photo, checked by fixed rules, with the fee released only if the photo passes.

We start in Munich, where we can walk the stores ourselves. We do not claim coverage in other cities or countries today; that is on the roadmap, not in the product.

Scope

One shelf photo proves presence and the shelf price. It does not prove placement or share of shelf.

Field checks: what brands legitimately check

Brands pay for field visits to answer these questions. Shelfie's prototype covers product presence, shelf price and out-of-stock observations; dedicated promotion checks are a future extension.

  • A promotion you paid for is visible. Brands need to see whether a display or promotional price appears on the shelf. The prototype does not yet verify promotion displays.
  • The product is on the shelf. Availability and out-of-stock. If the product is missing, the checker photographs the empty slot with the shelf label and the brand receives an out-of-stock report.
  • What the shelf tag shows. The tag shows what the store currently displays for the product, so a brand can see what the shopper sees.
  • Competitor shelf prices. Publicly displayed shelf prices of competing products.

We do not set prices

Shelfie records product presence and publicly displayed shelf prices. Checking whether a paid promotion is visible is a future extension. Retailers set their own prices: in the EU, binding resale prices are prohibited (section 1 GWB, Art. 101 TFEU), and manufacturers may only recommend a price (source 8).

Why the spend matters

McKinsey reported in 2019 that consumer-packaged-goods companies worldwide invest about 20 percent of their revenue in trade promotions. In the same analysis, 59 percent of promotions lost money (72 percent in the United States). That statement is about profitability, not about promotions that did not run. We cite it to show how much money sits behind promotions, not to claim a specific execution failure rate. Source: McKinsey, 2019, source 1.

One secondary data point on why shelf checks pay: a 2025 field study of about 24,000 SKUs across 11 grocery stores found that inventory audits increased overall sales by 11%, as reported by Retail Insight Network in 2026 (source 2; primary study not opened). These figures explain the incentive to inspect shelves. They do not establish the return on investment (ROI) of a Shelfie check; that needs a pilot with brands.

Unit economics and market size

Everything in this section is an illustrative estimate from our pitch facts (slides/facts.tex). None of it is measured. Prices stay hypotheses until the planned brand interviews.

Illustrative arithmetic: 12 - 3.50 - 0.10 - 0.60. Excludes support, disputes, refunds and checks on rejected photos; actual unit economics are unmeasured. The 0.60 for escrow and fees is unconfirmed. These EUR amounts describe a future pricing hypothesis; the prototype pays test ADA with no monetary value.

Market size

Total addressable market (TAM) reference: in-store merchandising services are about $6 billion worldwide (Beroe estimate, 2023). This broad services market includes work beyond Shelfie's narrow check. We do not state a serviceable market, because we have no source for the share that is a simple presence-and-price check, and no source for Germany alone.
